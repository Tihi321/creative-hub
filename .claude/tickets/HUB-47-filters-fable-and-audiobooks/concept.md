## 1. The idea, chosen and argued

**The ONE idea: the frequency illusion** ("once you notice something, you believe it happens a whole lot"), explained by selective attention plus confirmation bias. Synchronicity is not a second idea in the story; it is the meaning people hang on the illusion, and it appears only in the debrief.

Your suggestion is right, with two corrections to the facts:

- **Zwicky coined "frequency illusion" (2005), not "Baader-Meinhof".** "Baader-Meinhof phenomenon" is older (1994, a reader of the St. Paul Pioneer Press, later identified as Terry Mullen) and is a misnomer (the name comes from a German terror group, with no link to the effect). Use "frequency illusion" in the story and debrief; mention the other name once in the debrief as what people also call it. The Oxford English Dictionary lists both as the same thing. (Source 1.)
- **Zwicky's own explanation is exactly the two mechanisms you proposed**: selective attention and confirmation bias. (Source 2.)

Why this idea and not the others in the PDF:

| Candidate | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Frequency illusion (selective attention + confirmation) | **Chosen.** It is what the user's description (once you hold a piece of information a filter switches on) actually is. One moment can be felt (the name starts to appear everywhere), and it has a clean, testable turn (count it, and count something you were not primed on). |
| Inattentional blindness (the gorilla) | The mirror image: you miss what you are not looking for. It is the reason the thing was invisible before, so it supports the turn but is not the idea. Keep it as one key fact and a Go deeper book. |
| Synchronicity (Jung) | Not supported as acausal connection; as a feeling of meaning it is exactly what the illusion produces. A story that taught it as an idea would teach something unsupported. Use it as the meaning people give. |
| Priming and associative coherence (Kahneman) | Real, but the PDF's claim that holding information "primes you to detect weak signals" overreaches, and famous social-priming results have failed to replicate (see section 4). Not the idea. |
| Network ripple (Christakis and Fowler) | Different idea (influence across three degrees), not about noticing. The PDF's "strangers reacting to the same ripple appear synchronised" is a stretch. Go deeper only. |
| RAS "filters 99% of reality" | Popular claim that does not hold up (section 4). Debrief line, not the idea. |

The pitch of the fable: the thing was there all along, at the same rate; what changed is you.

## 2. Concept card (HUB-45 format)

- **id:** `frequency-illusion`
- **name:** The frequency illusion
- **theme:** Attention, perception, judgment
- **definition:** Once you notice something (a word, a name, a kind of car), it seems to appear much more often than before, although it has not become more common; you have become more likely to notice it.
- **mechanism:** Two ordinary processes act together. Selective attention: we notice what is relevant to us and drop the rest, so something newly learned jumps out from a background that was always there. Confirmation bias: once it jumps out, each new sighting is counted as a hit and the days it does not appear are not counted as misses. The result is a rising tally and a feeling that it means something. The same pair of processes works for any new item you attend to, which is the test: pick another one, and it will start to appear too.
- **misconception:** "If it suddenly shows up everywhere, it must mean something (or it really became more frequent)." Usually nothing in the world changed; and a sign that works for any name you choose is not a sign.
- **key facts:** see section 3.
- **story seed:** see section 3b.
- **go deeper:** see section 5.
- **risk notes:** Health: none; the story must not treat anyone's belief as a symptom. Legal: invented people and places only; do not name Jung, Zwicky or any author in the story, only in the back matter. Accuracy: do not claim the effect is proved to cause anything; do not say the brain "filters 99%"; do not say synchronicity is disproved (say there is no good evidence for an acausal connection and that coincidences are expected). Plot: must not follow The Celestine Prophecy (see 3b).

## 3. Key facts (each from a source I opened)

1. In 2005 the linguist Arnold Zwicky coined "frequency illusion" in a Language Log post; the Oxford English Dictionary now defines it as a quirk of perception in which something one is newly alert to suddenly seems everywhere, and treats "Baader-Meinhof phenomenon" as the same thing, a name from a 1994 newspaper reader.
   - Source: Ben Zimmer, Language Log, 19 March 2019, quoting the OED entry: https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=42180 (**opened**).
   - Quote: "a phenomenon to which one is newly alert suddenly seems ubiquitous" (the OED definition as quoted there).
   - Not opened: Zwicky's original 2005 post ("Just between Dr. Language and I"); the old archive URL did not respond. The coinage and date rest on Zimmer's quotation of the OED.
2. Zwicky explained the illusion as the result of two well-known psychological processes, selective attention (noticing what is salient and disregarding the rest) and confirmation bias (looking for what supports our hypotheses and disregarding counterevidence).
   - Source: Arnold M. Zwicky, "Why are we so illuded?", Stanford University, September 2006: https://web.stanford.edu/~zwicky/LSA07illude.abst.pdf (**opened**, 490-word abstract).
   - Quote: "a result of two well-known psychological processes".
3. In the 1999 "invisible gorilla" experiment, 46% of 192 observers who were counting basketball passes failed to notice a person in a gorilla suit; people perceive and remember only what receives focused attention.
   - Source: Simons and Chabris, "Gorillas in our midst: sustained inattentional blindness for dynamic events", Perception 28, 1059-1074 (1999), author's copy: https://www.chabris.com/Simons1999.pdf (**opened**; the Sage page returned 403).
   - Quote: "46% failed to notice the unexpected event" (results section, all conditions).
4. Diaconis and Mosteller (1989) built a model for coincidences involving newly learned words, and found that, once coincidences with apparent causes are set aside, four principles (hidden cause; psychology, including memory and perception; multiplicity of endpoints; the law of truly large numbers) account for most of the rest, and "account for much of the force of synchronicity".
   - Source: Diaconis and Mosteller, "Methods for studying coincidences", Journal of the American Statistical Association 84(408), 853-861: https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1989.10478847 (**abstract only**, read through the OpenAlex record; the publisher page and JSTOR returned 403 or a challenge page).
   - Quote: "almost any outrageous event is bound to occur".
5. Jung defined synchronicity as a "coincidence in time of two or more causally unrelated events" with the same or similar meaning, an "acausal connecting principle"; it is his hypothesis, not an experimental result.
   - Source: C. G. Jung, Synchronicity: An Acausal Connecting Principle (first published in German in 1952), record of the Routledge and Kegan Paul English edition (1972): https://archive.org/details/synchronicityaca0000jung (**snippet only**: Open Library search-inside returned the sentence from this book; the book text itself would not open, 403; the Princeton University Press and Routledge pages did not give text either). The 1952 date is **inferred** from common bibliographies, not read on a page I opened.
   - Quote: "coincidence in time of two or more causally unrelated events".
6. The ascending reticular activating system (ARAS), the brainstem network usually called the RAS, mediates arousal (wakefulness); lesions of it cause coma. The classic 1949 study showed that stimulating the brainstem reticular formation changes the EEG from sleepy synchronised waves to fast activity. Neither paper describes a filter that discards most of what the senses report.
   - Sources: Edlow et al., "Neuroanatomic connectivity of the human ascending arousal system critical to consciousness and its disorders", J Neuropathol Exp Neurol 71(6), 531-546 (2012): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22592840/ (**abstract only**, read through NCBI E-utilities); Moruzzi and Magoun, "Brain stem reticular formation and activation of the EEG", Electroencephalogr Clin Neurophysiol 1(4), 455-473 (1949): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18421835/ (**abstract only**, same route).
   - Quote: "The ascending reticular activating system (ARAS) mediates arousal" (Edlow et al.).

Dropped or not opened: Zwicky's 2005 post; Nickerson (1998) confirmation-bias review (only its abstract opened via OpenAlex, `https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175`; Zwicky's own definition is enough, so it is not a key fact); Princeton and Routledge pages for Jung; the Penguin Random House page for The Invisible Gorilla (404). The Invisible Gorilla's author site (http://www.theinvisiblegorilla.com/) opened and confirms the book and its publishers (Crown hardcover, Harmony paperback).

- **Setting:** The Returned Mail Office of Kell, a damp brick hall with sorting benches, a ledger shelf, a tram line outside. Today.
- **The moment the filter switches on:** On a Tuesday a crate of undeliverable parcels is opened and one label reads "Orvane", a small inland town (invented) neither she nor Teodor can place. By noon the name is on the radio's weather, then on a tram destination board, a customer's mug, a crossword clue. By Friday she has seven sightings in a notebook and her grandmother's saying in her head. She asks for leave, buys a coach ticket with the money she had put aside for her roof.
- **The turn where she learns why:** Teodor sees the ticket and asks one thing: may we count something? First, he brings last year's ledger: the name "Orvane" appears eleven times, in her own handwriting; she had handled eleven parcels for that town and never noticed. Second, he picks another name off the same crate, "Pellam", which she has not heard before, and asks her to listen for it for a week; by Monday she has four sightings of Pellam too (a street on the tram map, a seed packet). The sign works for any name she chooses; what changed is her attention, and she had never written down the days Orvane did not appear. She feels the floor tilt, then laugh.
- **Ending (not a debunking):** The sign is gone; the wish stays. She keeps the ticket and pays the difference herself; she goes to Orvane for her own reason, the first leave in three years, and keeps a notebook with two columns, seen and not seen. One small coincidence stays honestly unexplained in the story (a stranger on the coach is called Pellam), and the debrief says why that is expected (law of truly large numbers).

The story's claim is the opposite of that book's: the strangers did not hold anything.
- **Rules for the writer (go into the card):** no real person, place or company; do not name the "Baader-Meinhof" group in the story; do not say or imply the brain filters a percentage of reality; the sightings must be ordinary and plausible; keep the coach coincidence small and not explained away in the text itself; no author or book named inside the story.

## 4. What science supports, and what it does not

- **Supported (good evidence):** Selective attention and inattentional blindness are well established; roughly half of observers missed the gorilla (Simons and Chabris 1999, opened). Confirmation bias is a well-documented family of effects (Nickerson 1998, abstract opened). Coincidences are expected: Diaconis and Mosteller show that four ordinary principles account for most of them (abstract opened). What is thinner: the frequency illusion is a descriptive label by a linguist that combines those two known processes; I did not find a dedicated controlled experiment on it under that name. I did not search exhaustively (no search engine was available), so say "described as the result of" rather than "proved".
- **Not supported:** Synchronicity as an acausal connecting principle. Jung proposed it in 1952 as a hypothesis (sources opened for the definition only as a snippet); I found no experimental evidence for it. Diaconis and Mosteller's abstract says their four principles "account for much of the force of synchronicity". Say: it is a hypothesis about meaning, not a demonstrated mechanism, and the coincidences it explains are expected from large numbers.
- **The RAS "99%" claim:** I found no source for a 99% figure or for the RAS filtering reality by relevance. The RAS (brainstem reticular formation) was shown in 1949 to control the arousal state of the EEG, and a 2012 review says the ARAS "mediates arousal"; it is about being awake, not about choosing what to notice. Filtering by relevance is done by attention networks in the cortex and thalamus (this last point is **inferred** from the general attention literature; I did not open a source for it, so keep it out of the story and mark it in the debrief as "attention involves the cortex and other regions" or leave it out). The "99%" is a popular self-help number; the debrief should call it that.
- **Other things in the PDF to correct:**
  - Kahneman's priming claim: the Gemini text says holding information "silently primes your subconscious to detect weak signals"; in the real book, priming is about words and ideas making related ideas easier to retrieve. One famous behavioural priming study (Bargh 1996) failed to replicate in Doyen et al., PLoS ONE 7(1):e29081 (2012), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22279526/ (**abstract only**: "our first experiment failed to show priming"). I did not audit Kahneman's own chapters.
  - The Celestine Prophecy is fiction, and a 1990s spiritual adventure novel, not evidence.
  - Connected: its subject is how health, habits and moods spread through networks up to three degrees (publisher page opened: https://www.hachettebookgroup.com/titles/nicholas-a-christakis/connected/9780316036139/); it does not say that strangers reacting to a hidden ripple "appear synchronised" in the sense the PDF suggests.
